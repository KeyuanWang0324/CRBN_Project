Overview

Can a molecular glue be built to degrade PPIL4?

A structure-based pipeline that designs candidate cereblon (CRBN) glue degraders of PPIL4, a spliceosome component, and ranks them by whether they can plausibly hold a CRBN–ligand–PPIL4 ternary complex together. 2,500 designed analogs enter; 20 come out with full docking, calibration, and provenance.

This is a structural-plausibility enrichment pipeline. It prioritises chemotypes that are geometrically consistent with forming a ternary complex. It is not a validated predictor of degradation or efficacy, and contains no experimental confirmation of PPIL4 recruitment, binding, or anti-cancer activity. Read the rankings as triage, not truth.

Background

The mechanism, and why PPIL4

A molecular glue degrader does not inhibit its target — it destroys it, by forcing a protein the cell had no reason to discard into contact with the machinery that discards things. (Not every molecular glue degrades: many, rapamycin among them, simply hold two proteins together.)

01

CRBN is a substrate receptor on an E3 ubiquitin ligase

Cereblon sits in the CRL4 complex, whose job is to tag proteins with ubiquitin and send them to the proteasome. Its pocket normally reads a small set of natural degrons — short signals on a protein that mark it for destruction.

02

A glutarimide warhead docks a small molecule into that pocket

Thalidomide-class compounds bind CRBN through a glutarimide ring — a mimic of the cyclic-imide degron CRBN evolved to read on its natural substrates. (A degron proper is a signal on the substrate protein, not on the drug.) Every candidate here keeps that ring fixed and varies only what is attached to it: it is the CRBN-binding handle, and changing it breaks the mechanism.

03

The rest of the molecule creates a new surface

Bound to CRBN, the compound presents a composite surface that neither protein has alone. A protein that happens to be complementary to that surface gets recruited — a neosubstrate, meaning a target the ligase would never normally touch. This is the glue: the drug does not bind the target on its own.

04

The recruited protein is ubiquitinated and degraded

Held against the ligase, the neosubstrate is tagged and destroyed. Catalytic, and it removes the protein entirely rather than blocking one of its functions.

Why this target

RAS-driven cancers may depend on efficient pre-mRNA splicing. Oncogenic RAS raises transcriptional load, and the spliceosome has to keep up. The asymmetry matters: “spliceosome addiction” is established for MYC, whereas for RAS this is emerging evidence and an argument by analogy — not a settled result.

PPIL4 is a spliceosome-associated peptidyl-prolyl isomerase — a cyclophilin-family enzyme carrying an RRM domain — acting in catalytic activation of the spliceosome rather than forming part of its catalytic core. Our own DepMap co-dependency analysis places it alongside the Prp19/NTC complex and the second-step factors DHX38 and CDC40. That is our analysis rather than an established result, and co-dependency is a weak signal for broadly essential genes — which PPIL4 is, as the boundary below says.

PPIL4 is a druggable glue target, and that is already proven. The CRBN–FPFT-2216–PPIL4 ternary complex has been solved experimentally (PDB 9DWV). The question is not whether PPIL4 can be glued to CRBN, but whether a selective glue can be found — FPFT-2216 also degrades IKZF1/3, CK1α, PDE6D and DTWD1, so it cannot test a PPIL4-specific hypothesis.

The honest boundary. PPIL4 is broadly common-essential and is not shown to be KRAS-selective in DepMap knockout data — nor are the validated splicing factors SF3B1 and RBM39, because full-knockout CRISPR cannot resolve this class of selectivity. Any tumour selectivity would have to come from partial-degradation dosing against RAS's transcriptional load, and that remains untested.

The calibration set, and the structure it is all scored against

Before any candidate is scored, the scale has to mean something. Three positives establish what a working glue looks like under this protocol; three negatives, each missing a different functional handle, establish what an inert molecule looks like. Together they set the two bars every result in this project is read against. The experimental 9DWV structure is the ground truth the whole method is checked against.

9DWV Experimental reference · not a prediction —

The experimental CRBN–FPFT-2216–PPIL4 ternary complex, PDB 9DWV. This is not a prediction: it is the deposited structure, with the real FPFT-2216 taken from the deposited chemical component rather than from any docking run. It is what every model on this page is scored against, and it is superposed into the same CRBN frame as the rest, so it can be compared directly against them. Its PPIL4 chain is short because only the RRM domain was resolved experimentally, while the docked models carry a full AlphaFold PPIL4.

Dry lab pipeline

From 2,500 designs to 20 ranked molecules

Each stage narrows the pool and feeds the next. Stage 08 is where the ranking is actually decided; everything before it is a filter, everything after is calibration and assembly.

01

Analog design

2,500 thalidomide analogs generated with the glutarimide degron fixed and the benzo-ring substituents varied.

02–03

CRBN-glue classifiers

Random-forest models on pooled ChEMBL actives, scaffold-split, to drop chemotypes that do not look like CRBN binders.

04

Vina docking

Each candidate docked into CRBN's thalidomide pocket and PPIL4's RRM interface; supplies the starting pose everything downstream uses.

05–06

Ligand-free ternary pre-filter

Coarse HADDOCK3 screens, retained only as a cheap viability check — see the structural-blindness finding in Results.

08

Ligand-inclusive 3-body docking — the ranking stage

CRBN, the ligand and PPIL4 docked as three separate bodies, with the ligand parameterised into the CNS force field so its real chemistry can differentiate candidates. ~70 minutes per molecule.

09

Scoring against the real structure

Every docked molecule re-scored against 9DWV, which is what exposed the DockQ calibration failure.

11–12

Buy list assembly

Ranking, calibration bars, decision rules and registry lookup composed into the final list.

07, 10

Support stages, not in the ranking path

07 extracts the top models for inspection; 10 is the second, independently built screen whose molecules appear here as the cross-check set. Neither feeds the ranking, so neither is renumbered.

Method, in full

Enough detail to re-run this. Everything below is pinned in the repository as executable artifacts rather than described in prose — the config, the restraint file, the seed and the input structures are all committed.

Analog library

2,500 analogs enumerated on the thalidomide scaffold. The glutarimide CRBN-binding warhead is held fixed — it is the part that binds CRBN, and varying it would break the mechanism — while benzo-ring substituents and the PPIL4-facing arm vary. Both the phthalimide (di-oxo) and isoindolinone (mono-oxo) cores are represented.

Chemotype filter

Two random-forest classifiers on pooled ChEMBL CRBN actives: one on the glue chemotype, one on CRBN binding under a scaffold split, so a molecule cannot be scored well merely by resembling a training compound.

This filter is weak and is treated as such. Its output is saturated — 0.82–1.0 across essentially the whole library — so it barely discriminates. It is used only to drop obvious non-binders, never to rank, and the per-candidate value is shown in each record as context rather than evidence.

Pose generation
(AutoDock Vina)

Every molecule is docked twice: into CRBN's degron pocket, with the search box derived from the co-crystal thalidomide position in CRBN–thalidomide–SALL4, and into PPIL4's RRM interface, with the box derived from 9DWV's real contact residues. Ten poses each.

The two pose sets are then searched jointly for the best-affinity pair whose ligand-contact atoms do not substantially overlap — a molecule cannot use the same face for both proteins at once. Neither protein's pose is privileged. The selected CRBN pose is what enters the ternary stage.

Ligand topology
(PRODRG)

CNS needs a topology and force-field parameters for any non-standard residue. HADDOCK3 can generate these automatically, but that path is broken in a way that matters: PRODRG renames the ligand's atoms and the automatic path never propagates the renaming back into the coordinates, so CNS aborts on unknown atoms.

PRODRG is therefore run explicitly per molecule, keeping its renamed coordinate file, and the resulting topology is passed to every module that runs its own minimisation — topoaa, rigidbody, flexref, emref — not just the first. Setting it only on topoaa fails later with missing Lennard-Jones parameters.

Ternary docking
(HADDOCK3)

Three separate bodies: CRBN (chain A), the ligand (chain C, resnum 900) and PPIL4 (chain B). Funnel: rigidbody at 1,000 models → seletop 200 → flexref → emref → clustfcc, with a CAPRI evaluation after each refinement stage. Roughly 70 minutes per molecule.

Putting the ligand in the force field is the entire point. The earlier ligand-free stages could only see which CRBN residues were marked active, so molecules sharing a contact set produced identical scores — the structural blindness described in Results.

Restraints

One committed restraint file for every molecule and control — 23 ambiguous interaction restraints: 13 from CRBN's degron pocket to the ligand, 8 from PPIL4's RRM interface to the ligand, and 2 reciprocal. Because the ligand is always resnum 900, the same file genuinely applies to everything.

This replaced a scheme that derived CRBN's active residues from each candidate's own Vina pose, which leaked pose noise into the ranking and meant no two molecules were docked under the same conditions.

There is no direct CRBN–PPIL4 restraint. The ternary complex is bridged only through the ligand, so the protein–protein interface has to be earned by the molecule rather than imposed by the setup.

Scoring

The ranking value is the mean HADDOCK score over the 10 best-scoring models of a run, not the single best model — one lucky model is not evidence. Reported alongside it: the spread across those ten (a tight spread means a convergent funnel), the fraction of all models falling in the largest cluster, buried surface area, restraint-violation energy and van der Waals energy.

Reference
structure

The 9DWV comparison structure is built by taking that entry's CRBN and PPIL4 protein chains only, relabelling them to this project's chain convention, and dropping DDB1, the ligand, zinc and waters — so the comparison is a straight protein–protein one. Each model has its ligand chain stripped before scoring, for the same reason.

Reproducibility

Seed pinned (iniseed = 42). Note that the parameter is iniseed on the sampling module — a top-level seed is silently ignored — and that even with it pinned, multi-core runs are not byte-identical, because CNS orders parallel models nondeterministically. Exact reproduction requires a single core.

Two environment constraints cost real runs before they were diagnosed: the run tree must not sit in a file-syncing folder, because the sync daemon races HADDOCK3's inter-stage handoff and crashes the analysis; and its path must be ASCII, because HADDOCK3 validates the run directory against a restricted character set and refuses to start otherwise.

Why the scores are comparable at all

A HADDOCK score only means something within one exact protocol. Two people running “the same” method produce numbers that cannot be ranked against each other. So the protocol is pinned to committed, executable artifacts rather than a prose description: one restraint file identical for every molecule, a fixed random seed, fixed input structures, a pinned HADDOCK3 version, and a fixed output schema.

The restraints deserve specific mention. Earlier versions derived the CRBN-side contact residues from each candidate's own docked pose, which quietly leaked pose noise into the ranking. They are now one committed file for every molecule and control, so the only thing that varies between runs is the molecule itself. There is also no direct CRBN–PPIL4 restraint: the ternary complex is bridged only through the ligand, so the protein–protein interface has to be earned by the molecule rather than imposed.

The three rules a candidate is judged on

Everything above produces numbers; these are the three gates applied to them. They are deliberately independent — each asks a different question, so a molecule can top one and fail the other two. Results shows how rarely all three agree.

Rule A · upstream

Is the starting pose credible?

Before any ternary docking, the molecule has to sit plausibly in CRBN’s pocket on its own. This asks whether its Vina pose ranks in the top half of the designed set and whether the CRBN and PPIL4 poses use different faces of the molecule rather than the same one.

top-half pocket affinity & pose overlap ≥ 0.70
Rule B · structure

Is the predicted complex well-formed?

A good score means little if the models disagree with each other. This combines buried interface area, how much of the sampling converged on one binding mode, restraint-violation energy and the spread across the ten best models, into one value scored against the candidate pool.

structure axis ≥ 0 (at or above the pool mean)
Rule C · the bar

Does it beat the best real glue?

The docking score has to be better than Z6466608628, the strongest positive control and the best published PPIL4-selective glue. It is also the only reference here size-matched to the candidates, so clearing it is not simply a size effect.

mean of best 10 < −125.61

Rules B and C are computed directly from the pipeline’s own outputs. Rule A is a reconstruction: the original selection was made by hand and its exact thresholds were not recorded, so the definition above was fitted to reproduce the earlier decisions and should be read as a description of them rather than the rule as first written.

The controls are the whole point of this chart. A negative control designed to be inert — the glutarimide CRBN-binding handle deleted, so it should not engage the degron pocket the way a glue does — scores −112.20, marginally better than FPFT-2216, the real drug this complex was solved with. So anything scoring above roughly −112 is indistinguishable from a molecule known to be inert, and “beats FPFT-2216” is a meaningless claim.

That is why the bar used here is Z6466608628 at −125.61, the strongest control in the set. Seven of the twenty candidates clear it.

What the ranking actually says

Four things the numbers support, and the limits on each. Correlations below are Spearman rank correlations, computed over the molecules named in each case.

1. The ranking axis is substantially a size axis. Across all 26 molecules plotted above, the docking score tracks heavy-atom count at ρ = −0.58 — bigger molecules score better. It is not purely size: TM-04-064-02 is the largest thing here at 43 heavy atoms and still sits near the bottom, so shape and fit do carry weight. But the effect survives even inside the candidate set, where every molecule falls in a narrow 30–34 atom band and the correlation is still ρ = −0.39. A candidate that ranks well has partly earned it by being large.

2. Which is why the Z6466608628 comparison is the one worth making. That control has 30 heavy atoms against the candidates’ 30–34, so it is the one reference on this page that is size-matched to what it is being compared against. Beating it is therefore not automatically a size artifact, in the way that beating FPFT-2216 (20 heavy atoms) or the stripped negatives (12–23) would be. Seven of the twenty clear it. It also happens to be the strongest published PPIL4-selective glue, so the bar is a real one rather than an arbitrary internal threshold.

3. The three criteria are close to independent, which is why so few molecules satisfy all of them. Docking score and the structure axis correlate at ρ = −0.08 across the eighteen designed candidates — essentially unrelated. Individually the criteria are not especially harsh (6, 9 and 7 of 18 pass each), but because they are near-orthogonal their intersection is small: five candidates satisfy none, eight satisfy one, one satisfies two, and four satisfy all three. The scarcity is a property of asking three unrelated questions at once, not evidence that the four survivors are exceptional.

4. Score and funnel quality disagree at the very top. The two highest-scoring molecules, RW_708 and RW_491, both fail the structure criterion. RW_708 has the widest spread across its ten best models of anything docked (sd 12.0) and ranks last of the eighteen on the structure axis: a strong average pulled up by scattered models rather than one well-determined binding mode. Cluster convergence and score in fact run slightly against each other across the set (ρ = +0.20). If only the score were used, these two would head the list.

One pattern that is suggestive but not established. All four molecules satisfying every criterion share the same mono-oxo isoindolinone core and the same phenylpiperazine arm, differing only in a single benzo substituent — dimethylamino, hydroxy, bromo, trifluoromethyl. None of the six di-oxo phthalimides satisfies all three. That reads like a preference for the mono-oxo core, but at this sample size it is 4 of 12 against 0 of 6, which a Fisher exact test puts at p = 0.25. It is a hypothesis to test with more molecules, not a result. Note also that the di-oxo compounds score better on average (−126.1 vs −124.5); what separates them is the structure axis, not the score.

Handed the real drug, does the protocol find the real structure?

The one molecule whose correct answer is independently known is FPFT-2216, the compound the CRBN–PPIL4 ternary complex was solved with. Running it through the identical pipeline reproduces the experimental architecture at DockQ 0.376 — above the CAPRI “acceptable” threshold of 0.23, with an interface RMSD of 2.07 Å and 64.7% of native contacts recovered.

That validates the method, and only the method. It is one data point, and it says nothing about whether any individual candidate is a real degrader. It is also partly circular: the PPIL4-side restraints are themselves derived from 9DWV's own contact residues, so PPIL4 lands near the true interface partly by construction.

Three results that cut against the pipeline

Each was found by testing a metric the project was already relying on. They constrain what the ranking above can be read to mean.

Discarded metric

A ranking column failed its own calibration

DockQ — a 0–1 score for how closely a model reproduces a known complex, where ≥0.23 is CAPRI “acceptable” — looked like a quality score. Measured across all 27 molecules docked under the protocol, two negative controls placed 2nd and 3rd, above every candidate. Its correlation with the docking score is +0.055. It is now kept as a geometry diagnostic and is barred from the ranking.

Retired stage

An earlier stage was structurally blind

The first ternary stage never placed the ligand in the force field, so its output was fixed by the contact residues alone. Three separate pairs of different molecules returned byte-identical scores. It was replaced by a stage that docks the ligand as a real third body.

Failed cross-check

Two independent screens disagree

The second screen ranked its own candidates highly. Three were re-docked here under one protocol — two appear in the shortlist, the third fell below the cut — and all three scored worse than every candidate and worse than the noise floor. The same molecule scores ~14.5 units better under the other protocol, so the two score sets were never comparable. Read the ranking gap carefully though: those three carry no PPIL4-facing arm and are 20–24 heavy atoms against the candidates’ 30–34, so the size bias accounts for part of it. The protocol offset is the solid half of this finding.

Are these compounds purchasable?

Mostly no. Exact-structure lookup against PubChem returns a registry entry for only 3 of the 25 compounds on the order sheet; the rest are novel designs with no CAS number, so acquiring them means custom synthesis. The list also contains five pairs of constitutional isomers, which is why every row is pinned by InChIKey rather than by name or formula.

What this does not show

No experimental validation of these designs. None of the 20 candidates ranked here has been made or tested — every number on this page is a structural proxy. PPIL4 itself is not an unvalidated target: FPFT-2216 and Z6466608628 are published CRBN glues that act on it, and the ternary complex is solved (PDB 9DWV). A PPIL4-selective glue has also been reported — Z6466608628, one of the controls on this page — but it degrades PPIL4 only modestly and its authors present it as a lead for optimisation. That, rather than the absence of any selective compound, is the gap this project is aimed at.

The score is size- and interface-biased. Larger molecules with more surface to bury score better regardless of merit, and the control set does not remove that — it only makes the numbers comparable across runs. One piece of luck: the bar itself, Z6466608628 at 30 heavy atoms, is size-matched to the candidates (30–34), so clearing it is not purely a size effect. The noise floor is not size-matched — the degron-dead negative has 23 heavy atoms and FPFT-2216 only 20 — so part of why they sit low is simply that they are small.

The positive control is partly circular. The PPIL4 restraints derive from 9DWV's own contacts. A neutral-restraint control, testing whether the protocol finds the interface unaided, has not been run.

The biological hypothesis is unproven. PPIL4 is broadly common-essential and is not shown to be selectively required in RAS-driven cancers. Any tumour selectivity would have to come from partial-degradation dosing, which remains to be tested.

Team

Why we took this on

Cereblon molecular glue degraders are still a young field. A handful of important examples are known, but the chemical space around them and the set of proteins they could reach are both largely unmapped, and most proposed compounds have never been made or tested. That gap is what made this feel like something we could actually contribute to rather than only read about — the bottleneck is candidate molecules that someone has bothered to evaluate.

What draws us to degraders specifically is that they remove a protein instead of blocking it. An inhibitor has to find a pocket and occupy it, which leaves out the many proteins that have no good pocket, and it silences only the site it covers. Degrading the protein takes away everything it does at once — its scaffolding and binding roles as well as any enzymatic one. Glues reach that outcome with an ordinary small molecule rather than the bulkier two-headed designs that also achieve degradation, which is what makes them appealing as drugs.

PPIL4 came from the map, not from us. Petzold, Gainza and colleagues mined the CRBN target space computationally and then confirmed compound-dependent recruitment of a set of previously uncharacterised neosubstrates — PPIL4 among them (Science, 2025). That is what pointed us at this protein. Since then a PPIL4-selective glue has been reported: Z6466608628, which recruits PPIL4 selectively at EC50 0.34 µM but produces only modest degradation and is presented by its authors as a starting point for optimisation. It sits on this page as the control every candidate is measured against — and the honest statement of the gap is not that nothing selective exists, but that what exists is a lead worth improving on.

Keyuan (Ryan) Wang · personal note

What keeps me interested is the ceiling rather than the current state of the art. If a glue could be designed to order, the same mechanism would in principle reach almost any protein — including the ones no inhibitor can touch — and that would matter far beyond cancer. We are nowhere near that: which proteins can be recruited at all is still set by whether they present the right surface, and this project ranks structures rather than demonstrating degradation. But it is the reason the field seemed worth entering.
